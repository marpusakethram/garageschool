How to break into venture capital

There is no single path into venture, and most jobs are filled before anyone posts them. This guide covers the ways people actually get in, what firms look for, how the interviews work, and what to do over the next 90 days.

What the job is

From the outside, venture looks like picking winners. Day to day, it is mostly three things: finding companies before other investors do, deciding which ones to back, and helping the ones you back. Junior people spend most of their time on the first. Partners spend more time on the second and third.

The work is social. A typical week for an analyst might include fifteen to thirty founder calls, a few hours of research on a market, notes for the partners, and time at events or on outreach. If you would rather spend the week alone with a spreadsheet, other parts of finance may suit you better.

Venture firms are also small. Many have fewer than ten investment staff and hire one or two junior people a year. That is why referrals and timing matter so much.

Titles vary between firms, but the ladder usually looks like this.

The paths in

Junior investing roles

Some firms hire analysts and associates for two or three years, usually from investment banking, consulting, or startups. Larger firms sometimes run a recruiting cycle. Smaller firms hire when a need comes up, often through someone they already know.

That is the traditional route, and it still works. But it is no longer the only one. Today, almost anyone can end up in venture. There are more firms than ever, solo investors and small funds raise money on their own track records, scout programs hand out checks, and angel syndicates let people back startups with a few thousand dollars. Company data, market research, and the investors themselves are a search or a post away. No one is going to stop you because of where you went to school or which bank you worked at.

What decides whether you get in, and stay in, is whether you are the right person. That has two sides.

Technically, you need to understand something deeply enough to judge it. That could be how AI models are built and deployed, how hospitals buy software, how defense procurement works, or how consumer brands grow. Founders can tell within minutes whether an investor really understands their product. Firms want people who can spot a strong technical team, see through a demo, and know which metrics matter in a market. You also need the basics of the job itself: reading a cap table, sizing a market, writing a clear memo. The GarageSchool course covers those.

Personally, founders have to want you around. The best founders choose their investors, so you need to be someone they trust: honest when it is uncomfortable, quick to help, and reliable on small promises. You also need the judgment to say no most of the time and the conviction to say yes when others hesitate, plus the stamina for a job where results take ten years to show. Curiosity, taste, and a real network are hard to fake.

Get both sides right and the door is open, whatever your background. Get either one wrong and a good resume will not keep you there.

The operator path

Many investors join after years at a startup or a large tech company, where they became expert in something specific: selling to hospitals, building developer tools, growing a marketplace. Firms value this because it helps them judge deals in that area and helps the founders they back. This path is slower but often leads to more senior roles.

The founder path

Founders who built a company, whether it worked or not, understand the job from the other side. Many partners started this way.

After an MBA

Business school can help, mainly through the network and the structured recruiting some firms run. It works best if you arrive with a clear sector focus and use the two years to build relationships with founders and investors in that sector.

Portfolio, platform, and community roles

Portfolio, talent, community, and marketing roles at venture firms are a real career on their own. A portfolio associate learns how companies actually grow and how funds report to LPs. A community lead builds the relationships that bring the firm its best deals. Some people move from these roles into investing over time, but join because you want that work, not only as a back door.

The portfolio management course covers the work in detail, including portfolio and platform roles and community roles, with how to get hired into each.

Scouting and angel investing

If you know founders early, a scout program or small angel checks can start your investing record. Even a few small investments teach you more than reading about it, and they give you real deals to talk about in interviews.

What firms look for

  • Access to founders. Can you find good companies before other investors do? A network in a specific community, school, industry, or region is valuable.
  • Judgment you can explain. Firms want people who form a clear view and can defend it, and who change their mind when the evidence changes.
  • Depth somewhere. Knowing one sector very well beats knowing every sector a little.
  • Clear writing. Much of the job is writing notes and memos that partners read quickly. Short and specific wins.
  • Founders who like you. If founders want to take your calls, you will see better deals. Firms look for people who are helpful and easy to work with.

Build a record before you apply

The best way to show you can do the job is to do parts of it already. Before you start applying:

  • Write three to five investment memos on real private companies, using only public information. One page each: what they do, why it could be big, the main risks, and whether you would invest at a stated price.
  • Keep a deal log. Note companies you would back and why, with the date. Check back every few months. A log with honest misses is more convincing than a list of famous names.
  • Make a market map of a sector you know: the main companies, how they differ, and where you think the opportunity is.
  • Talk to founders. Offer real help, such as introductions, user feedback, or research. Some of these people will later be your best references and your first deals.
  • Practice decisions at volume. GarageSchool tests give you hundreds of decisions with known outcomes. Bring two debriefs to interviews: what you picked, what you missed, and what you would do differently.

A short public newsletter or blog on one sector works well. It shows your thinking, brings founders to you, and gives the firm something concrete to read before they meet you.

Getting in the door

Start with firms whose thesis matches what you know. If you spent three years in healthcare, look at funds that invest in healthcare. Read their portfolio and their partners' writing before you reach out.

Warm introductions work best, especially from founders the firm backed or wants to back. If you write cold, keep it short and specific: who you are, the area you know, one company you think they should look at and why, and a small ask. Sending a good deal is the strongest first message there is.

Do not ask for a job in the first message. Ask for fifteen minutes of advice, or share something useful. Follow up when you have something new, such as a memo, a market map, or a company worth meeting.

The interview process

Processes differ, but most include some version of these steps.

StageWhat happensHow to prepare
First conversationBackground, why venture, why this firm.Have a clear story for why you, why now, and which area you know best.
Pitch a companyYou present a private company you would invest in.Pick a company that fits the firm's thesis and is not already famous. Know the risks.
Market map or sourcing exerciseMap a sector or show how you would find deals in it.Name specific companies and explain how you would reach the founders.
Memo or caseWrite a memo on a company the firm gives you, often in a few days.Practice beforehand. State a recommendation and the conditions that would change it.
Partner meetingsDiscussion with several partners, often about a live deal.Ask good questions. Show how you think, not how much you have memorized.
ReferencesThe firm calls people who know you, including some you did not list.Tell your references what the role is and what you would like them to speak to.

Questions that come up often

  • What company would you invest in right now, and at what price?
  • Tell us about a deal you would pass on and why.
  • Which of our portfolio companies would you not have backed?
  • How would you find the best founders in your sector before other firms do?
  • Walk us through a market you know well. Where is the money made?
  • What have you been wrong about, and what did you learn?

Expect some numbers. Early-stage firms rarely ask for detailed financial models, but they do expect you to work through ownership, dilution, and return-the-fund math comfortably. Lessons 8 and 10 of the GarageSchool course cover what you need.

A 90-day plan

WeeksFocus
1 and 2Read the GarageSchool course, starting with lessons 1, 8, and 10. Pick one or two sectors to focus on. Finish Analyst tests 1 to 5.
3 to 6Write one memo a week. Talk to at least ten founders in your sectors. Keep a deal log. Finish Analyst tests 6 to 15.
7 to 10Build a market map. Publish two or three short pieces. Start the associate track. Make a list of 20 firms that fit your focus and find a warm path to each.
11 to 13Reach out. Practice pitching a company in five minutes. Do a mock memo under time pressure. Review your weakest GarageSchool debriefs and the lessons they point to.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Saying you are passionate about startups without showing anything specific you have done.
  • Pitching a company that has already raised hundreds of millions. It shows you follow the news, not that you can find deals.
  • Not knowing the firm's portfolio before the meeting.
  • Claiming more credit for deals than you had. Venture is small, and people check.
  • Treating the process as a one-time application. Most people get in through relationships built over months.

Getting into venture usually takes longer than people expect. The work you do to get ready, such as memos, founder relationships, and a record of decisions, is the same work the job asks for. None of it is wasted.
